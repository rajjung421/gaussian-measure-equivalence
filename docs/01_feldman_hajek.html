01 · Feldman–Hájek: when are two Gaussian priors "the same kind of thing"?¶

Companion to notes.tex, chapter 1. Every figure in the notes is made here (saved to figs/).

The question. We have a reference prior $P$ (a stationary SPDE) and an alternative $Q$ (a different parameter, an added source, a changed coefficient...). In finite dimensions they always have a density ratio $dQ/dP$. On a function space, i.e. as the mesh is refined, that ratio may stop existing. Then $P$ and $Q$ are mutually singular: they put their mass on disjoint sets, and one perfectly resolved sample tells you which one generated it.

The one-sentence intuition we will build: two Gaussian measures are equivalent iff you cannot tell them apart from a single infinitely-resolved sample.

Plan

  1. Finite dimensions: equivalence is the default.
  2. Infinitely many coordinates: Kakutani and the $\sum t_j^2$ condition.
  3. Feldman–Hájek in general (in the notes; here we just use it).
  4. Random functions: why changing the range $\kappa$ is harmless but changing the amplitude $\tau$ is not.

def bayes_error(lP, lQ):
    # error of the best one-sample test 'say Q iff log dQ/dP > 0', equal prior odds
    return 0.5 * ((lP > 0).mean() + (lQ < 0).mean())

1 · Finite dimensions: equivalence is the default¶

$P=\mathcal N(0,1)$, $Q=\mathcal N(0,1.5^2)$. The density ratio $\frac{dQ}{dP}(x)=\frac{1}{1.5}\exp\!\big(\tfrac{x^2}{2}(1-1.5^{-2})\big)$ is finite and positive for every $x$: every set that is impossible under one is impossible under the other. Equivalent.

The only way to be singular in finite dimensions is for one measure to be degenerate: $Q=\mathcal N(0,\mathrm{diag}(1,0))$ lives on the line $\{x_2=0\}$, which has $P$-probability zero.

2 · Infinitely many coordinates: equivalence can break¶

Take $x=(x_1,\dots,x_n)$ with $P=\mathcal N(0,I)$ and $Q=\mathcal N(0,\mathrm{diag}(1+t_j))$. The log likelihood ratio of one sample is a sum of independent terms

$$\ell_n(x)=\log\frac{dQ}{dP}(x)=\sum_{j=1}^n\Big[-\tfrac12\log(1+t_j)+\tfrac12\,\tfrac{t_j}{1+t_j}\,x_j^2\Big].$$

For small $t_j$: the gap between its mean under $Q$ and under $P$ is $\approx\tfrac12\sum t_j^2$ and its standard deviation is $\approx\sqrt{\tfrac12\sum t_j^2}$. So the two histograms separate like $\sqrt{\sum_j t_j^2/2}$: if $\sum t_j^2=\infty$ one sample decides between P and Q with certainty.

First the i.i.d. case $t_j=0.2$ for all $j$ (every coordinate has 20% more variance).

In [3]:
def llr_samples(t, reps, under, rng, chunk=500):
    # log dQ/dP for P = N(0, I), Q = N(0, diag(1+t)), on `reps` draws from P or Q
    t = np.asarray(t, float); a = t / (1 + t)
    const = -0.5 * np.log1p(t).sum()
    sd = np.ones_like(t) if under == "P" else np.sqrt(1 + t)
    out = np.empty(reps)
    for i in range(0, reps, chunk):
        k = min(chunk, reps - i)
        out[i:i + k] = const + 0.5 * (rng.standard_normal((k, t.size)) * sd) ** 2 @ a
    return out

With $n=1$ the best test is barely better than a coin. With $n=10^4$ coordinates it never errs: from one sample you just compute $\frac1n\sum x_j^2\to 1$ or $1.2$ (law of large numbers). In the limit $n\to\infty$, $P$ and $Q$ live on the disjoint sets $\{\frac1n\sum x_j^2\to1\}$ and $\{\frac1n\sum x_j^2\to1.2\}$: mutually singular, even though each coordinate is a harmless 20% change.

Now let the perturbation decay with $j$. Three choices:

  • $t_j=0.2$ (i.i.d.): $\sum t_j^2=\infty$, fast;
  • $t_j=j^{-1/4}$: $\sum t_j^2=\sum j^{-1/2}=\infty$, slowly;
  • $t_j=1/j$: $\sum t_j^2=\pi^2/6<\infty$.

Kakutani's theorem (1948) says a product of equivalent measures is equivalent iff the product of the per-coordinate Hellinger affinities $\rho_j=\int\sqrt{p_jq_j}=\big(2\sqrt{1+t_j}/(2+t_j)\big)^{1/2}$ stays positive; since $-\log\rho_j\approx t_j^2/16$, that is again exactly $\sum t_j^2<\infty$.

Read it: for $t_j=1/j$ the overlap (b) and the test error (c) level off — adding more coordinates never makes the two measures distinguishable. That is equivalence. For $t_j=j^{-1/4}$ they keep falling (slowly) to zero: singular.

2b · Means: the Cameron–Martin condition¶

Same idea for a mean shift $Q=\mathcal N(m,I)$: $\ell_n(x)=\sum m_jx_j-\tfrac12\|m\|^2$ is exactly Gaussian, $\mathcal N(\mp\tfrac12\|m\|^2,\|m\|^2)$, so the best test has error $\Phi(-\|m\|/2)$. Equivalent iff $\sum m_j^2<\infty$ — the shift must lie in the Cameron–Martin space of $P$.

4 · Random functions: range $\kappa$ versus amplitude $\tau$¶

Now the coordinates are the sine coefficients of a random function on $[0,1]$ with $u(0)=u(1)=0$: $$(\kappa^2-\partial_x^2)\,u=\tau\,\xi \quad\Longrightarrow\quad u(x)=\sum_j c_j\sqrt2\sin(j\pi x),\qquad c_j\sim\mathcal N\!\Big(0,\ \frac{\tau^2}{(\kappa^2+\lambda_j)^2}\Big),\quad \lambda_j=(j\pi)^2 .$$ This is the 1D version of our reference prior. Both $P$ and $Q$ are diagonal in the sine basis, so everything from §2 applies with $$1+t_j=\frac{\text{var}_Q(c_j)}{\text{var}_P(c_j)} .$$

  • Range change $\kappa_0=6\to\kappa_1=2$: $\;t_j=\big(\tfrac{36+\lambda_j}{4+\lambda_j}\big)^2-1\approx\frac{64}{\pi^2j^2}\to0$, $\sum t_j^2<\infty$ → equivalent.
  • Amplitude change $\tau_0=1\to\tau_1=1.3$: $\;t_j=0.69$ for every $j$ → singular (it is §2's i.i.d. case).

Draw all three with the same white noise and look.

The surprise. In (a) the range change ($\kappa=2$, green) looks by far the most different — much bigger, smoother excursions. The amplitude change (orange) looks like the reference with a slightly larger scale. Yet the green one is equivalent and the orange one is singular.

Equivalence is decided by the fine scales (high modes), because there are infinitely many of them. Large-scale differences, however dramatic to the eye, live in finitely many modes and can never be confirmed with certainty from a single sample.

4b · What you can learn from one sample (microergodicity)¶

The flip side: from one sample resolved to $N$ modes, fit $(\tau,\kappa)$ by maximum likelihood. $\tau$ is pinned down by the fine scales and becomes exact as $N\to\infty$. $\kappa$ is not — its Fisher information stays bounded. 300 replicate samples from the reference.

The spread of $\hat au$ falls like $1/\sqrt N$ (0.26 → 0.01), while the spread of $\hat\kappa$ stalls at about 2.5 no matter how fine the sample: the fine scales say nothing about $\kappa$. That is why $\kappa$ is a "cheap" direction for a stationary fit: moving it costs almost no likelihood.

Take-aways¶

  1. Equivalence = "same null sets" = a density $dQ/dP$ exists = one perfect sample cannot tell them apart.
  2. Diagonal case: equivalent iff $\sum_j t_j^2<\infty$ (variances) and $\sum_j m_j^2<\infty$ (means). Feldman–Hájek is this, written basis-free: $T$ Hilbert–Schmidt + mean in Cameron–Martin.
  3. It is decided at fine scales. Big, eye-catching large-scale changes (range $\kappa$, smooth sources, lower-order terms) are harmless; uniform small-scale changes (amplitude $\tau$, the leading coefficient) are fatal.
  4. Practical meaning: what a fine sample determines exactly ($\tau$, $K$) you cannot "correct" with finitely many directions; what it never determines ($\kappa$) is a cheap knob a fit can slide along.
